# DIVERTED — design notes

> *"british airways chatbot, british airways email, and british airways SMS: one always tells the truth, one always lies, and one"*
> — the thread, tweet 29 of 67, unfinished

This document explains what the game is trying to do and why each part of it is built the way it is. Where a mechanic lives in a particular file or function, it says so, so that the explanation can be checked against the code.

## 1. The premise, and what the source thread gave us

The brief: a horror game where your London–LA flight lands in Reykjavík with no explanation and no plan, cryptic messages and mystery buses follow, and the company threatens to strand you if you complain. Wrong mail, wrong bus, wrong complaint: game over.

The thread is not a horror story. It is a very funny, very tired account of a real diversion. But read as *system design* it is almost a finished game document, because the author keeps noticing the same three things:

1. **Information does not come from where it should.** Official channels (email, SMS, chatbot) are late, contradictory or physically impossible ("at 9:40 we received an email that said buses would pick us up at 9:00"). The reliable sources are *a water-stained A4 printout in Arial* and *random passengers conveying hearsay*.
2. **The only way to know you are in the right place is to recognise other passengers**, "despite not being very good with faces", and to notice that they are wearing the same clothes as last night.
3. **Authority is polite, imperious, never sorry, and openly threatening** ("I am in charge and the safety of our customers is tantamount"; "he'd strand people in Iceland if they resisted or objected").

Plus one line that is literally a UI spec: *"imagine a video game meter but for my nerves, ticking down into the 'thin shaking sliver of red' zone."*

Every mechanic in the game is one of those observations, made literal. The horror is not added on top of the comedy; it is what the comedy is already about, with the reassurances removed.

### Thread → game beat map

The game opens forty minutes before the thread does, at the gate, because the horror needs a *before*: a cabin that is normal for long enough that the player has a seat, a neighbour, a meal and a routine to lose. Every element of the later threat is planted there in an innocent form — the phrase in the safety demonstration, the headcount card, the purser's habit of looking at people rather than paperwork — so that when the threat comes it is recognised rather than introduced.

## 2. What was borrowed from the two reference games

### From *No, I'm Not a Human*: screening by tells

The core loop of that game is: something arrives at your door claiming to be what you need; you inspect it for small physical tells; you let it in or you don't; the rules for what counts as a tell are drip-fed through an unreliable broadcast; being wrong is fatal; runs are short and the endings are collected.

DIVERTED maps this one-to-one onto buses:

- **The visitor is a coach.** Each bus scene (`buses1`, `buses2`, `bsi`, `lind_buses`, plus the single-bus traps `decoy_morning`, `lind_decoy` and `walk`) presents 1–3 vehicles with a name, a sign, two visible details and two or three **hidden details** revealed by *Look closer*, which costs minutes. Boarding is the irreversible act — and so, at the night stand, is dithering. Once you are late there (the bathroom, 02:20, or the empty hall), the plain coach gives you eight minutes: *Run for it* gets it; looking closely at it puts you at its door, where the driver says *In or out? I'm going* and you choose; looking closely at either of the others takes long enough that it leaves while you are reading, and you are left with the crest and the city bus (`bus1_door`, `BUS1_CHECK`). The morning stand is far more forgiving — the plain coach waits until 11:25, so looking costs you nothing — but a player who keeps looking, or keeps waiting for the time on the sign, can lose that one too, and is left with the crest and the turquoise shuttle.
- **The tells are diegetic and consistent across the whole game.** The right bus always: has a handwritten or felt-tip sign (Arial/paper, never LED); has a driver in hi-vis who doesn't care about you; carries people you recognise, in the clothes they flew in. The wrong bus always: has the crest and the LED; has a driver in the *vanished crew's* uniform who is smiling at you specifically; carries rested strangers in clean shirts, who have no phones out. The third option (Flybus, Blue Lagoon shuttle) is the genuine-but-not-yours bus, whose tell is that its passengers have luggage or clean socks — things you conspicuously lack. At BSÍ, where you are a city passenger like anyone else, the Flybus with the luggage is the honest one, and the clerk's card says so.
- **The broadcast that teaches the rules is hearsay.** In NINAH it's the TV. Here it is the Icelander, the man in the fleece, the woman with the toddler, the man in the Blazers cap, the taxi driver, and an anonymous +354 text: "dont get on the nice one". Talking to people raises a hidden collective count and unlocks hint lines under the scene text. The official channels, by contrast, are the *lie* channel. The player has to learn that the logo is the tell for danger — inverting the trust hierarchy the airline's branding is designed to install.
- **Details are randomised per run** (order, livery, wording of the paper sign) with a seeded RNG, so the answer can't be memorised as "the left one"; it has to be *read*. `G.pick` / `G.shuffle` in `content.js`.
- **Permadeath, short runs, an endings gallery.** A run is 20–30 minutes. Six endings, two good, stored in `localStorage` and shown as locked cards with a one-line hint so the gallery itself is a soft guide ("It had a crest on it. It was very nice." / "He did say."). §6b describes them.

### From *Don't Look Outside*: the room, the needs, the rule

That game's contribution is the shape of the middle act: you are confined; you have small mundane needs (eat, sleep, keep it together); night is the danger window; and there is a stated rule that the whole game is really about whether you will break it.

- **The rooms.** The hotel is four hub scenes — `room`, `corridor`, `lobby`, `carpark` — you move between, plus the road to the 10-11. The action lists *are* the thread's inventory of small miseries: no toothpaste, the tiny shampoos, crisps-and-mini-wine, looking up your rights, posting about it, the ice machine, the night clerk's crossword. Nothing here is a puzzle; it is texture, and every action returns a different paragraph (§5), so the same room is never the same room twice.
- **The needs are not a survival sim.** There is no hunger clock. Food, tea, a shower, other people and sleep matter only because they pull the two gauges down a little (§4b); doing nothing is always allowed and nothing here starves you.
- **The rule.** "Please do not look out of the window", delivered by the chatbot at 04:50, is the one explicit *Don't Look Outside* homage. Looking is *not* fatal — that would be a cheap trick — but it sets `seen`, which the purser references later, on the tarmac bus: "You looked." The cost is dread carried forward, not a game over. The knock at 04:30 ("Coach for Albion Atlantic passengers. Departing now. Last call.") is the night's real test: it is the *chatbot's* stated time, and only the paper sign contradicts it.
- **Night as the danger window.** Every lethal hotel choice is nocturnal (the walk, the knock — which finds you wherever you are, in the room or in the corridor — the coach in the car park, the lift that arrives on its own; the window is the sub-lethal one). The morning is comparatively safe until the buses come, which is how the reference game paces it too: chores, then the door.

## 3. The three channels ("one always tells the truth, one always lies, and one")

The thread never finished the sentence. The game does, roughly:

| Channel | Behaviour | How it's built |
|---|---|---|
| **Mail** (Albion Atlantic, with crest) | Always *late* and about the wrong thing. Sent-time and received-time are shown separately so the player can see the 09:00 bus email arriving at 09:40. | `G.at()` with `stamp` earlier than delivery; the email view prints both. |
| **Ally** (chatbot) | Always *lies*, but specifically and usefully: it names the trap you are about to walk into (04:30 coach, 08:00 transfer, "room 214, do not look out of the window"). Its questions change with the situation: before the diversion it will only tell you the flight is on time and sell you wifi; after it, it has opinions about your hotel and your bus. Complaining to it changes nothing except how you feel. | `CONTENT.chat` — each question has an `if` and an answer that is a function of state. Everything you can say to Ally is said inside the chat panel; the room never offers "text the chatbot" as an action. |
| **SMS** | Sparse. Two from the airline (your seat, then the wrong hotel), one from a friend (hot springs), one from an unknown number (right). | Plain bubbles; the unknown number is the game's only unambiguous hint and it is deliberately deniable. |
| **Paper** | *The truth.* Anything printed in Arial and taped to something. The player photographs it and it goes in a tab rendered in actual Arial with a water stain. Paper is a thing you hold, so it arrives even when the phone is dead, and the Paper tab is the one tab that still opens on a dead phone. | `renderPaper()` in `engine.js`; `.paper-sheet` in CSS. |

The design goal is that by the second bus scene the player is checking the Paper tab first and reading the crest as a threat. That reversal — the branded, designed, professionally worded channel is the dangerous one; the ugly hand-made one is safe — is the game's thesis and it comes straight from tweet 32: "hundreds of other people who choose to trust the waterstained arial font printout over the emails with the BA logo, because we all know better."

## 3b. Reading

The phone is the game's only honest instrument, and the one thing the room will never do is read it for you, or tell you to. When something on it is unread, the hub text *implies* it and nothing more — the phone lit face up and has been for a while; a buzz against your leg; somebody else's phone chiming with the same chime as yours, then yours; a notification light at the edge of your vision — drawn from a small pool so the hint is never the same sentence twice. What is on the phone, you find out by opening it. This is enforced in two directions:

- **Information opens options.** The purser at the aircraft door asks for your seat before you have looked at anything. The seat is in the welcome email and in a text; he says *"Take your time. It will be on your phone,"* and waits. "31B" becomes an answer once you have read either; "thirty-something" gets corrected and noted; and a player who is still looking after two tries is told the seat off his own card and let through anyway — the airline never refuses you boarding, it only remembers. The booking page is reachable only from the button inside the accommodation email. "Go outside and look for the 08:00 coach" exists only once you have read the chatbot's 08:00, and says 09:00 only once you have read the email that says 09:00. The crested car outside Lind can be got into only once you have read the email that sent it; before that the street merely has a black car in it, and the text says so. In the taxi, the email you cannot show on a dead phone is still a clue if you read it at passport control.
- **Information constrains options.** The compliant gates — the ones that take away a defiant option when dread is high — apply only once the instruction they enforce has been read. *Please do not look out of the window* closes "Look" at dread 90 only if you opened the message; a player who left the phone face down can still look, frayed or not, because nobody has told them not to. The same holds for "don't open" at the knock (gated by the 04:15 "a member of staff will knock") and for pulling the charger out at Lind (gated by the chat line that names your room). Messages carry a `key`; reading one sets `read:<key>`; a choice with `instr: <key>` is gated by dread only when that flag is set. Reading something re-renders the room's text and options in place. A dead phone receives nothing and therefore forbids nothing.

## 4. Complaining

The brief says *make the wrong complaint* is a fail state. The thread supplies the taxonomy:

- Complaining **to a person in a uniform** — in the cabin, at the bag counter, at the one counter, at the gate — is **NOTED**. One pip each. The purser writes on a small card. Complaining to the chatbot is not noted: it costs you a little composure and nothing else, because a chatbot cannot see your face, and the airline acts on faces.
- At three, the airline acts on it *where it can* — at the check-in counter or the gate, never in the hotel. At the airport, complaining is also a visible staircase: the first option is "find someone in a uniform and tell them exactly what you think"; the second is "find the uniform again — he wrote it down last time", and it costs dread as well as nerves, so the ladder is felt as well as read; the third says that security has been looking over since the second, and taking it is being escorted out. With nerves at the top of the bar the option collapses to "tell him, in the voice you have left", which is the third step in one. There is one way down from there that is not a complaint: sitting on the floor against a pillar with your eyes closed, which takes nerves off and is closed only to the very cowed, so a frayed player is not simply handed to security by the clock. The kiosk, which the thread tried twice, can be tried three times.
- Being noted three times is not the ending; it is a scene (`left_behind`). The uniform turns over the card, says the line from the cabin without any unkindness, and waits to see what you do. You can accept it. You can shout. Or you can **argue your case, calmly, and all of it** — and that option is open only if your nerves are low enough to stay calm (≤70) *and* your dread is low enough to argue with a uniform at all (≤65). Pleasant and non-compliant, both: the game's definition of the person who gets through. Argued well, one tick is crossed out and you go on to the gate with two. Otherwise two people in black walk you through a door you did not know was a door, and you are landside, in the hall you arrived in, with time to try the doors, ask the man in hi-vis what you are supposed to do now, look for the others through the glass, phone Jo, and sit down on the floor to wait for a desk that opens at six.
- Complaining **to each other** — comparing notes, sharing the UK261 QR code, laughing at the gate agent, telling the truth to the man who argued your case with you — is counted silently. It is the only kind that helps. It unlocks hints, and it is what you have in hand at the gate, where the airline makes its last move (§4d).

There is one hidden complaint: confirming the Heathrow hotel then *not* getting in the car sets `booked`, and the check-in agent later notes that "our records show you were accommodated" — a strike you earned twelve hours earlier by trusting an email. That's the "trust the wrong mail" consequence for players who dodged the immediate one.

## 4b. The two gauges: what they do and, more importantly, what they don't

- **Two gauges, both fill, neither ends the game.** NERVES fills with confrontation and stress; DREAD fills with compliance and with looking at things you were told not to look at. Small choices move them a few points. Time moves them a little (nerves +1 per 40 minutes awake; dread +1 per 30 minutes between midnight and dawn). Sleep, food, a shower, a laugh, and other people pull them down. Two of the domestic ones pull *both* gauges down, unevenly, so neither is a free reset: the shower most (−6 nerves, −3 dread), tea next (−5, −2). Trying to sleep rests the body and not the mind: −4 nerves, and +1 dread, because you lay there and it did not come (−2 nerves and nothing else once the knock is already on its way, because you were almost, almost asleep). The same small dread cost, one or two points, is attached to every option that turns out not to be available — looking something up on a phone that is dead, charging a phone with no charger, brushing teeth with no toothpaste, showing a QR code that is on a black screen: the option stays listed, because you would try, and trying costs. The springs are the exception that proves the rule: every dip takes nerves down and dread *up*. There is no "meter hits max → death" moment anywhere in the game.
- **Their only effect is to close options.** Every choice can carry a `nerveMax` or a `dreadMax`. When a gauge is past that number the option is still listed, but crossed out and disabled, with a one-line reason written for that option (`whyNot`, or `whyNotN`/`whyNotD` when the two gates need different reasons): a frayed player cannot lie still, would spill the tea, would snap at the man in the fleece; a cowed one cannot look up their rights, cannot turn their back on the coach, cannot walk toward the man knocking, cannot get out of the warm water. The reasons never name a gauge, but each family keeps its flavour — trembling and temper on one side, paralysis and obedience on the other — so they stay learnable. High nerves take away the calm, patient, sociable options and leave the confrontational ones. High dread takes away the defiant, independent options and leaves the compliant ones. So the gauges never kill you; they leave you with the choices you would have made anyway if you were that frayed or that cowed, and *some* of those are lethal.
- **No single failure point.** Thresholds are per option and deliberately uneven: talking to the older couple needs nerves ≤75, the fleece man ≤90; refusing the knock needs dread ≤80, backing away from the spyhole ≤88, walking to the 10-11 ≤70, the fire stairs ≤92, getting out of the springs ≤72, arguing your case at the counter ≤70 nerves and ≤65 dread. A player who has been mostly compliant all night arrives at the knock with dread in the eighties and finds that "don't open" is gone — but "look through the spyhole" is still there, and behind it one more chance. Every funnel has more than one wall, and every wall is in a different place.
- **Two kinds of option, two motifs, never labelled.** Confrontational options (`kind: 'conflict'`) carry the nerves bar's motif: a red left edge that thickens and a text-shadow that reddens as nerves rise, and at the top tiers they shake. Compliant options (`kind: 'comply'`) carry the dread bar's motif: the airline's navy wash and a gold glow that intensify as dread rises, a gold ✦ in place of the chevron, and the text grows a little. The bars use the same colours, so after a run or two the player can work out which bar governs which family — the game never says. That is the teaching loop the brief asked for: you learn across runs what kind of option each thing is, before you learn whether it is good or bad.
- **Neither family is "the bad one".** Some compliant options are correct (stay seated on the tarmac bus, board by group, don't look out of the window, wait for the counter); some confrontational ones are correct or harmless (object in the cabin, demand your bag, ask the driver). Some of each are game over (open the door, get in the lift, board the crested coach; demand to be let off the tarmac bus). The motif tells you the *temperament* of a choice, not its outcome.
- **Repetition is cheap.** Doing the same thing twice in the same place costs half as much on the gauges (`engine.js`, `choose()`), so a player who checks the door four times is anxious, not doomed.
- **Tuning.** Automated playtest policies (`tools/playtest.js`: compliant, confrontational, random, "sensible", and a hand-written careful route) run in all four languages. A careful run ends around nerves 0–10 and dread 60 with no option ever gated; a purely compliant run reaches the knock with dread 70–100 and is usually funnelled into THE CREW; a confrontational run reaches nerves 75–100, loses the ability to laugh at the gate or wait at the counter, and collects NOTED pips until check-in. Random explorers who poke every dark corner saturate both bars by 04:00, which is the intended punishment for treating the hotel as a checklist.

## 4c. The phone

The battery is a number in the corner of the phone, and it is the third gauge, the one the game never calls a gauge. It starts at 31% at the gate, loses a point every half hour of play and every twelve minutes after midnight, and loses more when you use it — posting, looking up your rights, asking Ally things. On an ordinary night at Hraun it dies somewhere between half past three and four, in the room, an hour before the knock.

- **The death is a scene, not a status change.** When the battery reaches nothing, the next time you are in the room or the corridor the game takes over: a full-width picture of the phone, 1%, the screen going to the colour of the room and then to the colour of nothing, a descending tone and a click, and one option, dread-coded, the only one there is. The scene knows what you were doing when it happened — the engine remembers the last choice and the scene it was made in — so the phone is face up on the duvet if you were trying to sleep, in your hand after anything else, and lit by the exit sign in the corridor. Each version offers two things: go back to what you were doing (*Get up. Put the light on* / *Carry on* / *Stay out here*), or the bed (*Go back to sleep* / *Lie down* / *Go back to the room. Lie down*). The first is closed above dread 80, so a cowed player is sent to bed by their own feet — which is how the corridor's lift, and the knock, get their quietest customers. It costs dread on arrival and on either choice, because there is nothing else it can cost. The phone panel itself goes black, with a faint reflection and the words NO POWER, and the battery readout becomes a dash; only the Paper tab still opens, because paper is in your pocket, not in the battery.
- **A dead phone hears nothing.** Everything the airline sends while it is dark goes into a backlog instead of the inbox: no toast, no buzz, nothing. The 03:50 "are you comfortable" and the 04:15 "a member of staff will knock" arrive, if at all, at breakfast, when somebody at the next table has a cable that fits — which means that on most nights the knock at 04:30 is unannounced. That is the design: the phone is the one thing telling you what is about to happen, and the game takes it away just before it matters.
- **A phone that comes back brings everything at once.** Charging flushes the backlog in a single burst — a stack of notifications, a run of buzzes — with the original timestamps, so you can read the night you missed in order. At Hraun this is a few messages at breakfast. At Lind (§5d) it is the point of the branch.
- **Reading has a price.** Messages can carry a dread cost that is paid only when they are opened or the tab is viewed, never on arrival. The player who leaves the phone face down is better off than the one who reads everything, and the game never says so.

## 4d. The stand-off

The airline's last move is made at the gate, when boarding begins. It begins with people you have never seen: the crested coach's passengers, Tuesday's and Thursday's, in clean shirts, with navy folders and boarding passes for AB 0271 — one of them for 31B. The gate agent announces that customers who were accommodated will board first and the rest will be accommodated on a later service. Nobody says offloaded. The records say which of you are the passengers, and the records are in the folder. This is the conflict every tell in the game has been setting up — yesterday's clothes against clean shirts, faces against paperwork — and it is one that a single person cannot win, because a single person arguing it is NOTED.

The scene (`standoff`) is reached from every gate choice, and offers four things. Which of them work depends on what you did all night, in two different currencies:

- **Let the crowd find its voice** — say nothing yourself, look at the man in the fleece. If the collective count is 7 or more, he says *count us*, the mother says *same clothes as last night*, and the scene moves to its second beat (`standoff_crowd`): the man from 31C and the printout, somebody reading Article 4 off a phone if anyone looked it up — and then the airline answers. The jetbridge door opens and the purser from your flight walks out with his card, the clean shirts turn to look at him all at once and then at you, security appears at the edges, and he says the cabin line in the voice that has worked all night. You choose again: *hold* (closed above dread 88), *add your voice, quietly* (if you have the photograph or the regulation; closed above nerves 80) or *step back*. Holding or adding wins: a hundred people say true things in bus-stop voices until the mother asks *which customers?*, the toddler asks it too, the microphone goes down, and at the door the purser looks, for once, at the paperwork and makes no mark. THE COLLECTIVE. Stepping back is the thing that stops it — the fleece sees you do it — and the purser makes a mark instead. LEFT BEHIND. Below 7 at the first beat, a crowd that has not been talking is only a queue, and you are a remaining customer. LEFT BEHIND. The first beat is closed above dread 85, because you are looking at the floor.
- **Make a fuss** — loud enough that the whole gate hears it. Security takes your elbow, and the second beat (`standoff_fuss`) is the purser coming out of the door to say, pleasantly, to you, the line about customers who resist or object. The hand tightens. You choose: *keep going, louder, to his face* — with a collective of 10 or more they cannot move you (the fleece in front, the mother with both hands free, a hundred people in yesterday's clothes between you and the door, not touching anybody; the purser looks at his card and finds no number on it for this; *Karen*, said with affection) → THE COLLECTIVE; below 10 nobody is in front of you → LEFT BEHIND. Or *say his name*, Dev, the one name you learned, if you compared notes at breakfast or in the queue — a social save that needs only 8, because a name is a smaller ask than a riot and a larger one than silence; one name alone is not a crowd. Or *stop, let them walk you*. The first beat is closed above dread 80. The higher thresholds are the point: raising your voice is only safe if you spent the night earning the people who will stand in front of you.
- **Go up to the desk** — make your own case, quietly, whatever the collective. The desk turns the card over and counts the ticks; the records are read out (the Heathrow hotel, the unused accommodation, Hótel Hraun), and you answer the way you would answer a colleague, and mention the regulation if you have it. While she types, the purser comes out of the jetbridge door with his card, looks at you, looks at her screen, and goes back in. She prints 31B. HOME. Above nerves 70 it comes out harder than you meant and she makes a mark, which is the third if you had two. Closed above nerves 85 (you would not get through the first sentence quietly) and above dread 92 (the desk is where they write things down).
- **Wait for the later service** — the compliant option, always open, and the only one open to a player who is both frayed and cowed. The later service is tomorrow's. LEFT BEHIND.

So the good endings are earned in different places: the collective one in the lobby, the breakfast room and the queue, by talking; the lone one at the desk, by temperament. Showing the QR code around is one of the things that counts, and it is no longer enough on its own.

## 5. Text that never repeats

Hub scenes build their paragraph from three parts (`hub()` in `content.js`): a short fixed status line that says where you are and what is wrong with you (*"Room 214. 03:40. You are too tired to sleep, and your teeth are dirty, and you have not eaten."*), the outcome of what you just did (`G.note()`, set by the action), and one ambient line drawn from a pool for that place. Ambient lines carry a dread tier; the picker prefers lines at or just below the current tier and never repeats the last one used, so the same lobby at dread 2 is a crossword and a tour poster, and at dread 5 the clerk says "He asked for you" without looking up. Repeatable actions (checking the door, the television, the window, the ice machine, the vending machine) key their outcome on how many times you have done them and on dread, so the fourth door-check reads differently from the first. Nothing in a hub renders the same paragraph twice in a row.

Two rules for the text itself:

- **The prose never hedges on state.** Wherever the text depends on what the player did — the bag from the 10-11, the socks, the QR code, the man from 31C — it resolves the condition and says what is true, never "if you have it".
- **The prose never knows what the player doesn't.** The text may only use what *this* run has shown the player. The way out of the hall is worded by what you learned there (the fleece's pointing, the Icelander's "there are buses", the mother who seems to know where she is going, or just the drift through the door that says nothing); the hint under the coaches appears only if the mother said it; 216 is "the fleece man's room" only if you saw him get the key, and "next door" otherwise; the walk is to "the 10-11" only if the clerk named it, and to "the sign at the junction" if not; the morning coach you go out to look for is the 08:00 one until you have read the 09:00 email; the couple on the sofa are "the couple from the window" only if you met them in the hall; the car park's "Walk to the far end. Toward the shape." is only worded that way once the status line has put a shape there, and becomes "toward the coach" once you have seen it; the chatbot is called Ally in narration only once you have opened it. `G.did(key)` — a test for whether a once-only action has happened — is what lets content ask "did you talk to him" without a flag for every conversation. The test suite drives each of these branches both ways.

## 5b. Escalation

Dread also drives the presentation, in six tiers (`data-dread` on the root element, set from the gauge):

- The page darkens: the vignette closes in, the scanlines thicken, the title flickers faster, at tier 5 the scene text jitters, at tier 6 the location line turns red.
- The clock lies: from tier 3, every few seconds it shows `--:--` or a time from hours ago for a fifth of a second.
- The pictures degrade: from tier 3 a figure in navy with a white collar appears at the edge of scene vignettes, more often as dread rises; from tier 4 frames occasionally drop to black; the redraw rate climbs.
- The chatbot gets closer: from tier 3 Ally's answers gain a second line (*"You are still in room 214."*, *"Please remain where you are."*); at tier 5, *"Why are you still here?"*
- Daylight helps: sleeping (or failing to) at 07:30 takes 25 points off dread. The airport and the gate put some of it back.

## 5c. Four languages, four lanes

The game exists in English, French, Icelandic and Finnish. The choice is made in the game's first scene: boarding at Heathrow, four lanes, four signs (*Lane A · English / Voie B · Français / Rein C · Íslenska / Kaista D · Suomi*), a gate agent screaming about lanes — the mirror image of the Keflavík gate agent screaming about groups fourteen hours later. Picking a lane sets the language the airline has promised to serve you in.

Why these four, for the record: English because the source thread is English; French because the author is French and wants to show the game to his family; Finnish because the author, at the end of a long day, briefly believed Reykjavík was in Finland; Icelandic because Reykjavík is, on reflection, in Iceland.

Who speaks what is part of the design, not an accident of translation. The asymmetry is deliberate and there are only two rules:

- **The airline speaks correct English and bad everything else.** In the French, Icelandic and Finnish versions the emails, the SMS, the chatbot, the purser's announcements and the counter and gate staff are rendered in machine-translated, half-broken French, Icelandic or Finnish — English word order, wrong cases, "staff" and "lobby" and "transfer" left in English, "tantamount" left untranslated, the tagline calqued into *Nous faisons notre meilleur* / *Við erum að gera okkar best* / *Me teemme meidän parasta*. "We are proud to serve you in the language of your choice" is the airline's promise, and this is what the promise is worth. It is also a small horror device: the only voice with authority is the one that cannot quite speak to you.
- **The Icelanders speak correct English and correct Icelandic, halting French, and no Finnish.** The airport woman, the night clerk at Hraun, the clerk at Lind and the taxi driver are the honest voices, and they are at home. In the Icelandic version they speak proper Icelandic. In the French and Finnish versions they answer in English — a French or Finnish traveller in Keflavík would be spoken to in English — which is left untranslated inside the narration. In the French version only, you can ask whether they speak French (a real option at the airport and at either hotel's desk), and from then on every local speaks a halting, kindly French for the rest of the night (*« Onze. C'est écrit onze. Peut-être vous dormir. »*). There is no Finnish equivalent, because there is no reason an Icelandic night clerk would have any.

Everything else — narration, your choices, your fellow passengers, Tuesday's passengers at Lind, the backpackers — is simply in the language of the lane you chose, revised for naturalness by readers of each language (the French also follows the spaced en-dash convention, « – », never the em dash, outside the airline's own copy). All three should still be read by a native before anyone is shown them in earnest.

Mechanically, `content.js` is the source of truth; the other three files are generated from it by substituting every string literal through a dictionary (`tools/i18n.py`, `tools/dict_*.json`), so the four versions cannot drift in logic, only in prose. Lines spoken by locals are wrapped in `LX(...)` in the source; the generator leaves those in English for French and Finnish, and the French file carries a second small table (`tools/dict_fr_broken.json`) for the halting-French variant. The airline's lines are not marked in the source; the dictionaries translate them badly, on purpose, and a list of which keys are the airline's voice is kept with the tools (`tools/voices.json`) so a revision pass cannot accidentally fix them. The engine holds a `CONTENTS` registry and swaps the active one when a lane is chosen; the lane scene itself lives in `engine.js` because it is the one scene that has to exist before there is a language.

**The title screen** is the one place a visitor arrives before choosing a language, so it behaves like an airport information display: every few seconds it flips through the four languages — subtitle, blurb and buttons — with a split-flap animation, and stops flipping the moment a lane is chosen. Nothing on it says "select language"; it just keeps showing you your own until you board.

## 5d. The other hotel

The yellow Flybus at the coach stand is a real bus to a real place, and the wrong bus has its own night.

- **BSÍ at closing time.** The city bus terminal, 02:50, diesel and cinnamon, the kiosk pulling its shutter down, the backpackers walking away toward beds that are theirs — and in the far bay, engine running, interior lights on, a navy coach with a gold crest that was told you were coming. Your phone dies here, in your hand, with the terminal clock stopped behind it: a lower dread cost than the room scene, because it is not yet the middle of the night, but the same mechanism, and the same backlog starts filling. You can board the coach (THE CREW), sit on a plastic seat until a man in hi-vis tells you the taxi is outside, or find the taxi.
- **The taxi driver is the only person all night who takes you where you ask**, and the conversation with him is a small screening scene in reverse: he is screening you. He has done this run every night this week — same flight number, same hour, same crowd in the same clothes; he waits at the rank outside Arrivals every night and nobody from the airline has ever asked him to wait for anyone, or paid any driver he knows. Ask to show him the Heathrow email and you get the phone out and remember that it is dead; if you had opened that email at passport control you can half-recall it — a Renaissance something, Bath Road — and that is enough for him, because the people with that email are the ones the white bus takes to Hraun, so the useless email becomes the clue that gets you there. Ask him about the flight and he tells you the others go "somewhere out past the lava, a different hotel every night, I think". Getting him to take you *there* is possible but not free: the option appears only once he is talking, only if you can describe the white coach yourself (you looked closely at it at the stand, or somebody in the hall told you something), only if your nerves are low enough to be civil to him, and it costs the fare. Miss any of that and he takes you to the nearest place with a light on.
- **Hótel Lind** is a narrow guesthouse off Laugavegur that the airline never booked, with a clerk who has a paperback, a kitchen with the light on, and a drawer full of cables left by every guest who ever went home. Everything Hraun refused is here for the asking, on arrival or at the desk later: toothpaste (half a tube, from a man from Düsseldorf), a tray of bread and skyr, a charger, and a handwritten card with the Flybus times on it and a warning you did not ask for: *the yellow one, not the other one*. Room 7 has a window onto a street with people in it, which is its own kind of wrong at four in the morning, and the clerk will unlock the door and let you stand in that street, and lock it behind you, and watch you through the glass. The driver has a name, Gunnar, and the clerk knows it: he has brought her one of you every night this week. The branch's horror is the inverse of Hraun's: there you are confined and provided for by something that knows your room; here you are free and comfortable and *it still knows your room*.
- **The charger is the trap.** Plug the phone in and it comes back at full, and the night you missed arrives all at once: thirty-odd mails, texts and chat lines, timestamped across the dark hours, that guilt-trip you for making alternative arrangements, threaten to record you as a no-show, demand your location, then simply state it — *You are at Hótel Lind, room 7* — and arrange your "transfer to your accommodation" for 04:30. Each carries a dread cost paid only when read. The scene gives you three ways out: start reading, put it face down and let it charge, or pull the cable and leave it dead, which costs nerves and is the sanest thing in the room. Starting to read opens the phone and moves you to a scene with no way out but the phone: *Put it down* appears only once the unread count is zero, so the cost of the flood is paid message by message, through the phone, and not by clicking a button that says you read them. Until then the one option is *Stop reading*, which costs a little nerve and is closed above dread 60: a calm reader can put it down half-read; a cowed one has to read to the end. The flood is where the branch's pressure comes from, and it pushes in three directions at once: the 04:30 "transfer" (the knock); an email that says a car is waiting outside and means it — a black car with a crest at the kerb, your name spelled right on a tablet, the clerk shaking her head through the glass, and THE CREW if you get in; and the sane-sounding one, giving up and going to the right hotel after all, which the clerk will arrange by phoning Gunnar, who drives you out to Hraun in time for its knock. The knock at 04:30 comes regardless, two flights up a building the clerk locked at midnight, and the voice says the room number in case you had forgotten it.
- **Morning at Lind** is the driver's week made visible: the long table by the window is Tuesday's passengers and Thursday's, in clean shirts and clean socks, looking at their phones, which confirm every morning that the transfer is at nine. They are lovely. They have been rested since Tuesday. The clerk puts out more skyr the way you would feed something you had decided to keep. The compliant option, confirmed by every official channel, is to wait for the transfer with them; at 10:15 it comes, a navy coach filling the street from wall to wall, and boarding it is THE CREW — you become one of them. The right move is the thing the clerk told you: ten minutes' walk to BSÍ in daylight, a ticket from the kiosk, the yellow bus with luggage on it. You rejoin at the airport an hour early with a full battery, clean teeth, dread high, and a check-in agent who frowns at the screen because your accommodation was somewhere else.

Gauge-wise the branch runs nerves down and dread up, which is the funnel into its ending: the lenient hotel is the one that leaves you most cowed by morning.

## 5e. The springs

Jo's text, the turquoise shuttle, the mentions: everyone says the hot springs are twenty minutes from the airport, and they are. From either hotel's morning, or from either morning coach stand, you can go (`springs`). It is a place, with the same shape as the hotels: a status line (how long until check-in closes, whether you are in the water), an outcome, an ambient line from a pool that fills with Tuesday's passengers as dread climbs, and a set of small actions.

- **Every dip trades nerves for dread.** Getting in takes twelve points off nerves and puts four on dread; staying in takes seven and puts seven; floating with your eyes closed takes eight and puts eight. It is the only place in the game where feeling better is the thing to be afraid of, and the gauges say so without a word.
- **Getting out is gated by dread — once you are in.** "Get out. Find the shuttle back to the airport" needs dread ≤72; past that the reason reads *You cannot get out. The water is warm, and you are very tired, and nobody has asked you to.* A visitor who never got in is only *leaving*, and can, at any dread: the water is the trap, not the place. A player who arrives frayed and dips once or twice can still leave, colder and relieved, and rejoin at the airport. A player who floats until the dread is high cannot, and the next "stay in" is LAZARUS — as is the flight itself leaving while you are still in the water, which the scene checks on every return, so that looking at the other bathers for long enough is also a decision.
- **The clock still runs.** Check-in closes an hour before departure. If it closes while you are in the water, a shuttle goes without you — "the one that mattered" — and getting out afterwards is LEFT BEHIND, in the car park, in the plane clothes, smelling of eggs.

## 6. Tone

The thread is deadpan, and deadpan is the correct register for dread. So:

- **The reason for the diversion is never explained beyond what the thread had.** The thread only ever has it second-hand: "a medical emergency", a purser implying the passenger is in first class, and at the end "allegedly in intensive care — I do not know how trustworthy that update is". The game keeps exactly that: the captain says a customer is unwell, a curtain is drawn, if you walk past it you see someone on the galley floor and a blanket and are not told what is wrong, and the good ending wishes the customer from the forward cabin well, on hearsay. Nothing is ever confirmed, and no ending explains it.
- **No jump scares, no gore, nothing supernatural is ever confirmed.** The crew "vanish". The coach passengers are "rested". The carpet is wet. The purser looks up at your window. That is as explicit as it gets. The player's imagination does the rest, which is cheaper and scarier.
- **The airline's voice never changes.** Every threat is delivered in customer-service English. "We're doing our best" appears in the cabin, in every email footer, at the coach door in the dark, and at the check-in counter. The last one is in daylight and it is meant to be the worst.
- **Real jokes are kept intact** — the second kiosk, girl dinner, the wifi refund — because they are what a person actually notices at hour 26, and because the laughter *is* the survival mechanic. Every laugh in the game takes a few points off both gauges.
- **Iceland is not the threat.** The thread is emphatic on this and so is the game: the Icelander is the first honest voice, the 10-11 is "lit like a shrine", the taxi driver is the only person who takes you where you ask, the sheep are very nice.

## 6b. The endings

Six, and each is a different way of losing (or not), rather than a different place to lose in. Where the same loss can be reached by several routes, the opening paragraph says which route you took and the rest is shared, so the player always knows *what* happened to them and never has to guess whether two endings were really the same one.

- **THE CREW** — *you went with them.* The coach with the crest at the night stand or in the morning; the knock answered, in the room, in the corridor, in the car park or in the street at Lind; the car with your name on a tablet, at Keflavík or at Lind's kerb; the coach you walked into on the road to the 10-11; the coaches "organised for you" in the empty hall; the confirmed transfer from Lind with Tuesday's passengers. The ending is about the other passengers: they turn to look at you all at once, the way a field turns in wind; nobody has a yesterday, or a phone; the man beside you says, without turning, *"We were told you were coming."* Then the windows go dark the way windows do when there is nothing outside them, and you can feel yourself starting to smile. The mechanism it names is compliance: every route into it is a "yes".
- **LEFT BEHIND** — *the flight left and you did not.* Three complaints noted and a case not argued, or argued badly; the exact printed time waited for at the sign or at BSÍ; the shuttle that mattered missed from the water; the tarmac bus demanded off, into a lay-by with sheep. The shared middle is the airline's records, which are what happened: you were accommodated, a car was waiting, coaches were organised, a member of staff knocked, your feedback was noted. The end is tomorrow: the same flight will come in at one with two hundred people with no coats, and there will be a hotel and a knock and a coach for the patient ones, and you know, now, how to be patient. The mechanism it names is the airline's memory, and the loop.
- **LAZARUS** — *you stayed in the water.* Only from the springs, at high dread. Not a decision like the others but the noticing of something already true. The 11:00 bus leaves without you; the emails arrive to a phone in a locker; for the first time in two days you are not holding anything; the woman who has been here since Tuesday floats past; a man in navy stands on the far side of the water with his hands folded on the surface, looking at the sky. The mechanism it names is relief as the trap.
- **THE LIFT** — *you got into the lift that came on its own.* Only in the corridor, at dread tier 4 or above. The numbers go 2, 1, 0 and stop being numbers; hold music; the posh voice thanking you for your patience; doors that open onto rows of seats. The mechanism it names is the building itself being the airline's.
- **THE SELF-GOVERNING COLLECTIVE OF LHR–LAX** — *you made it, together.* Only through the stand-off (§4d), and only because the others carried it: the crowd that found its voice, or the crowd that stood between you and security. The opening says which. The mechanism it names is the thread's own: hearsay, faces and a printout beat the records when enough people say so at once.
- **HOME (OR GREENLAND, OR HELL)** — *you made it, alone.* You went up to the desk yourself and made your own case, quietly, and she gave you 31B, and you did not look round. Rows one to twenty are clean shirts. The mechanism it names is that the records can be argued with, one passenger at a time, by someone who does not push; it costs nothing you can see.

Each ending has its own music, written for what the ending does rather than for how sad it is (§7b).

## 7. Visual design

Both reference games are low-resolution, murky, and let you *look* at the thing you are deciding about. The graphics here follow that, with one constraint: no image files. Everything is drawn procedurally in `art.js` into a tiny canvas (160×72 for scenes, 128×64 for buses) and scaled up with `image-rendering: pixelated`, so the repo stays a handful of text files and the look is consistent at any size.

- **Scene vignettes.** Every scene and ending carries an `art` key that picks a painter: the cabin with its lit seatbelt sign, the fridge-lit terminal with the door that says nothing, the sodium-lamp coach stand, the road through the lava, room 214's window (which shows the coach in the car park only if you have looked), the corridor with the wet carpet, the lobby with the A4 sign, the departure board with one red row, the jetbridge that ends in a bus, the pastures, the aircraft in the rain; the phone, very large, dying or filling; BSÍ with its one lit bay and the crested coach in the dark; room 7's window onto a street, with the man under the lamp from dread tier 4 and the coach wall-to-wall once you have looked; the guesthouse with the clerk, the kitchen door and the leaflet rack; the springs, pale water and steam and lava, with still heads in it and, from tier 4, one in navy. They redraw twice a second with a new seed, so tubes flicker, rain falls and people shift — the *Don't Look Outside* trick of a still frame that is not quite still. Redraws stop under `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- **Bus portraits.** Each coach card has an illustration generated from a small spec (`livery`, `windows`, `passengers`, `sign`, `driver`). This is the *No, I'm Not a Human* peephole: the tells are visible before you read a word, if you know to look. The crested coach has warm, bright windows and one identical upright silhouette in every window. The plain coach has dim windows and passengers slumped at different heights, some windows empty, one with two people. The Flybus has luggage racks. The lagoon shuttle has towels. The driver wears a hi-vis, a navy jacket with a white collar, or nothing in particular. None of this is labelled; a second run teaches you to read it.
- **The frame.** A single dark theme, deliberately: it is 2am in a closed airport in every scene. Surfaces are a warm near-black with a 4px dither pattern; panels have a two-pixel bevel with a hard shadow, like a 1990s dialog box that has been left in a smoking room. Body text is set in VT323 (a terminal face) at 21px for legibility, labels and titles in Press Start 2P, both from Google Fonts with Courier fallbacks. Both gauges are segmented bars: nerves in red, shaking at the top; dread in the airline's navy-to-gold, glowing at the top. A full-page vignette darkens the corners and faint scanlines sit over everything. The phone keeps its own modern system-font styling on purpose: it is the one clean, corporate, well-designed object in the game, and it is the one lying to you.

## 7b. Sound design

Everything is synthesised in `audio.js` with the Web Audio API — noise, a few oscillators, filters, two feedback delays for a hall and a comb for metal — so there are still no media files. Sound starts on the first click (browsers require a gesture) and a ♪ control in the top bar mutes it, remembered between visits. The title screen says the rest in one line: best played with earphones, and the sound on.

Each scene key has its own soundscape, crossfaded over two seconds when the scene changes:

- **Cabin**: a 55 Hz engine drone under brown noise, a faint hiss, and every so often the seatbelt chime — the "spoon on a glass" — which also fires on entering the diversion scene.
- **Heathrow gate and jetbridge**: a hall murmur, a two-tone PA chime now and then.
- **Keflavík arrivals**: fluorescent buzz at 50 and 100 Hz, a faint hall wash, the tube above the bag counter ticking every few seconds, and once in a while the floor-cleaning machine passing (a slow swell of filtered noise).
- **Coach stand**: wind (band-passed brown noise with two slow LFOs), a 32 Hz diesel idle with a square-wave chug, rain.
- **The road**: engine drone, road noise, a slow rumble.
- **Room 214**: the heating at 60 Hz, a low wash, and every half-minute or so a small click from the corridor.
- **Corridor**: near silence, the ice machine grinding for two seconds at a time, and, rarely, the lift cable — a three-second triangle tone.
- **Lobby**: the coffee machine's hum, the glass doors sliding open for nobody.
- **Car park**: harder wind, gravel.
- **The phone**: almost nothing — the heating far off and a thin 11 kHz tone that is the phone itself, until it isn't.
- **BSÍ**: a big cold hall with one diesel idling and a tannoy that says something once. **Room 7 at Lind**: wind in wires, a car that does not slow, a gull. **The guesthouse**: a fridge, a clock, somebody else's kettle.
- **The springs**: water moving, a great deal of air, voices far off in steam, and every so often a shuttle that comes and goes.
- **Departures**: murmur, a fluorescent edge, the split-flap board flipping (fourteen clicks), the PA chime.
- **The tarmac bus**: engine and rain on a roof. **The apron**: rain on metal and a jet spooling.
- **The void** (the empty title vignette): a 30 Hz drone and a slow heartbeat thud.

On top of the scene, **dread** adds a detuned pair of sine waves around 40 Hz whose level rises with the tier (silent at tiers 0–1, audible from 2) and whose pitch drops as the tier climbs; from tier 5 a barely-there 9 kHz whine joins it. From tier 4 it gets bells: far off and slow, clusters of detuned inharmonic partials with a soft attack and a six-to-ten-second decay, a slow tremolo on the body so each one seems to turn as it rings, a brown-noise clapper. They are tuned to each other — a low D, its tritone, the minor second above, and their octaves — so that when two ring together (from tier 5) they make a chord nobody would choose, and at tier 6 a D an octave down joins them, almost too low to be a note.

**Nerves** get percussion. First a heartbeat, low-passed and felt more than heard, which the nerves tier (0–4, one per 25 points) drives the same way the dread tier drives the drone. Tiers 0–1: nothing. Tier 2: a slow lub-dub at 60 beats a minute, low in the mix. Tier 3: 78, louder, and a dry tick between beats — a nail on a tooth, a pen on a table. Tier 4 (the bar full): 96, louder again, and wrong — beats stumble, double, or the whole thing runs for a bar and settles. The pulse never stops by itself; only the gauge coming down slows it, so sleep, tea and the shower are things you hear working. Then, from tier 3, the building: a mining bar dropped on a rail in a large industrial space that keeps the sound. The hit is a burst of bright noise with a hard edge, soft-clipped, a low dead knock from the bar, and a 3 ms feedback comb that puts the rail's metal into it; around it, chains — a scatter of small bright clicks that settle after the hit like links coming to rest — and sometimes only the chains, dragged. All of it goes through a hall made of two feedback delays that darken each pass, so every hit has a room around it. Irregular, never on a beat; at tier 4 louder, often bouncing twice, and sometimes in angry runs of four to six, as if somebody downstairs were hammering at something that will not give. The reference is the kitchen-and-dungeon ambience of *Fear & Hunger*: industrial, physical, somebody else's violence heard through a floor.

Each layer works alone: the bar and chains over a quiet dread are a frayed person in a calm building; the bells over a calm pulse are a cowed person in a wrong one. Together, at the top of both — drone, heartbeat at 96 with its ticks and stumbles, bells turning in the hall, the bar on the rail and the chains settling — they add up to a single, very high-stress piece of music that nobody wrote. A limiter on the master keeps all of it from clipping.

One-shot cues are fired by the engine: a two-pulse buzz for every message, five even thuds when a knock scene opens (it is the same knock whether you are in the room, the corridor, or two flights up at Lind), a square-wave blip when the airline notes you, a descending tone and a click when a phone dies, a run of fourteen buzzes when one comes back with the night's backlog, and the split-flap clatter when the title board changes language.

**Each ending has a theme** (`AUDIO.ending`), a short procedural piece in the mood of what the ending does, not a sting:

- *THE CREW*: the dread bells, descending by whole steps, over a drone a semitone below everything else, and slow swells of darkness with no road in them.
- *LEFT BEHIND*: the terminal — fluorescent hum, a flat 55 Hz that does not move, and a tannoy two-note that never gets its third.
- *LAZARUS*: water and breath, a warm major third that swells and never resolves, and a heartbeat that slows from sixty and stops.
- *THE LIFT*: the lift bell, a sawtooth drone that goes down by semitones for longer than the building has floors, and hold music in triangle waves.
- *THE SELF-GOVERNING COLLECTIVE*: rain on a staircase, voices, and a chord people make by accident — a stack of fifths, warm, a little out of tune.
- *HOME*: the cabin drone, and the seatbelt two-note that finally gets a third, upward.

## 8. Interface

- **Two panes: the world and the phone.** The thread was written *on a phone, in the situation*, so the phone is a permanent second character. On desktop it is a sticky column with a battery that runs down over the day; on mobile it is a bottom sheet behind a PHONE button with an unread badge. Toasts announce arrivals so the player feels the buzz at the same moment the narrator does. The chatbot lives inside it, and only inside it.
- **The departure-board aesthetic.** Amber on black, monospace, a faint scanline, a title that flickers every few seconds. Email gets the airline's navy-and-gold header; the chatbot gets rounded bubbles and emoji; paper gets Arial and a stain. Each channel *looks* like its level of trustworthiness, which is the joke.
- **Sound control**: the ♪ in the top bar. Off by default only if you switched it off last time.
- **Business Class**: offered twice, in fiction — by a woman with a clipboard halfway down the jetbridge, and by the welcome email. Accept either and nothing changes about your seat, but the bus cards lose all their written tells (name, sign, the Look-closer lines) and leave you the pixel portraits alone to read, with one italic line in the airline's words where the name was — "Business Class customers are trusted to find their own way" — so the blank reads as the choice you made, not a fault. It costs a little dread on acceptance. It is the difficulty mode, chosen in-fiction and never named as one.
- **The clock is always visible** because every horror beat in the source is a timing beat (the email at 9:40 about 9:00; the counter at exactly 3 hours). Time only advances through choices; there is no real-time pressure, so reading is always free.
- **The dead phone**: a dark slab with a faint reflection and NO POWER; its tabs stop working until the phone is charged, except Paper. A new run also starts with a clean phone: nothing that was open on the screen at the end of the last one survives the title screen.
- **Accessibility:** pixel fonts fall back to Courier if Google Fonts is unreachable; body text is 21px for the terminal face's legibility; no information is carried by colour alone; `prefers-reduced-motion` disables all animation including the vignette redraws; `aria-live` on the status bar and toasts; keyboard-focusable choices; canvases are `aria-hidden` because every visual tell is also written on the card.

## 9. Technical choices

- **Vanilla HTML/CSS/JS, no build.** The requirement is GitHub Pages; the simplest thing that deploys there is static files, and a text game does not need a framework. `engine.js` knows nothing about the story; `content.js` is the story and knows nothing about the DOM; `art.js` and `audio.js` know nothing about either and only paint, or play, what they are asked to.
- **Scenes are plain objects with function-valued fields.** Text, location, choices and even bus lists can be `(G) => …` so they react to time and flags. This keeps conditional narrative in one place instead of scattering scene variants.
- **A tiny scheduled-message queue** (`S.sched`, drained by `advance()`) is what makes the "email arrives 30 minutes after you boarded" gag work mechanically rather than as a line of prose.
- **Seeded RNG per run** (`mulberry32`) so a run is reproducible from its seed if you ever want to add a "share this run" feature.
- **`localStorage` only for the endings gallery, a run counter and the last language**, guarded with `try/catch` for private windows. No save-in-progress: runs are 20 minutes and permadeath is the point.
- **The battery lives in the engine, the drama in the content.** `advance()` drains `S.batt` with the clock; `G.batt(d)` for use; `G.dead()`; `G.charge(pct)` revives it and flushes `S.backlog`, into which `deliver()` and `G.bot()` divert everything while the phone is dead (paper excepted). Messages may carry `dd`, a dread cost paid by `costOf()` when they are read, and `key`, which `costOf()` turns into a `read:<key>` flag that content and the `instr` gate consult. Content decides when the death becomes a scene (`phone_dies` at Hraun, the BSÍ text in the city) and what the flood says (`lindFlood()`).
- **Endings are six objects with route flags.** Every call that ends a run sets a `via_*` flag and names one of six ids; the ending's text is a function of those flags, so a new route into an existing loss is a new opening paragraph, not a new ending.

## 10. The design notes themselves

This document is part of the design, and it is delivered like the game: `design.html` is generated from the Markdown in the game's own style, exists in all four languages, opens in whatever language the interface is showing when you click the link on the title screen, and carries a visible switcher at the top (EN · FR · ÍS · FI). The translations were made with the same tooling and the same revision pass as the game text; the English is the source of truth, and where they disagree, the English is right.
